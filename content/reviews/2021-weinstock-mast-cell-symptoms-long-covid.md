## Study design

This is a cross-sectional online survey with three groups. People with Long Covid filled in the
same symptom questionnaire twice, at one sitting: once for how they felt before their COVID-19
infection, from memory, and once for how they feel now. Two comparison groups filled it in
once. The first was a "general population" control group of hospital employees and their
spouses who said they had never had overt COVID-19 symptoms. The second was 80 patients the
lead author had diagnosed with mast cell activation syndrome (MCAS) in the previous two years.
They were asked to describe their symptoms before MCAS treatment, from memory.

The questionnaire is built from the mast cell mediator release syndrome (MCMRS) form
(Appendix 4), with extra questions on common Long Covid symptoms, the Fatigue Assessment Scale
(FAS) and a 0–100 self-rated health score (Appendix 1A and 1B). The paper derives three summary
scores from the MCMRS items: what the methods call the "total number of MCMRS symptoms", a
"cumulative severity" (the sum of all 1–10
severity ratings) and a count of body systems affected.

The authors state three aims: that before COVID-19 the Long Covid group was "starting from a
baseline similar to healthy controls", rose significantly after COVID-19, and was "ultimately
reaching a level that resembled MCAS patients".

No biological samples were taken from the Long Covid group or the controls. The paper says so:
"laboratory and biopsy findings were not measured" in those groups. Every finding about the Long
Covid group is a self-reported symptom.

Quotations below keep the paper's own abbreviations for mast cell activation (MCA), mast cell
(MC) and Long Covid (LC).

No protocol or registration is cited. The paper describes no power calculation.

## Sample selection

**Long Covid group (n=136).** Recruited through Long Covid Facebook support groups, with an
advert promoting "an investigation of symptoms present before and after COVID-19 infection".
The paper gives no definition of Long Covid. It does not report how long people had been ill or
how long ago they were infected, so there is no way to tell whether everyone met even a
three-month threshold. 110 (80.9%) said their infection was confirmed by a test; the rest said a
physician diagnosed it without testing. 22 (16.2%) were hospitalised. The paper then gives this
as "19 diagnosed with testing and 2 clinically diagnosed", which adds up to 21, not 22. All of
this is self-report.

The paper does not report whether participants had ME/CFS, postural orthostatic tachycardia
syndrome (POTS) or post-exertional malaise (PEM). It does not report severity beyond the fatigue
and health scores. The authors acknowledge that support group members "might be more ill than
other patients".

**Controls (n=136).** Employees of Missouri Baptist Medical Center and of the lead author's
offices, and their spouses, recruited through an intranet post and handouts. They were included
if they had never had overt COVID-19 symptoms; no antibody test was done, so some may have had
an unnoticed infection. Chronic illness was allowed, deliberately: "In this manner we did not
exclude MCA symptoms in this group." That is a reasonable choice for a comparison group. But
the controls differ from the Long Covid group in sex (65.4% women against 89.7%) and in body
mass index (25.5 against 28.2). Neither difference is adjusted for in any analysis, so
part of the gap between the groups could come from who was in them rather than from COVID-19.

**MCAS patients (n=80).** All diagnosed by the lead author at a gastroenterology clinic that
specialises in MCAS. The diagnosis used a version of the "Consensus-2" criteria, tightened by
the authors to require symptoms in five or more organ systems. It also required at least one
raised mast cell mediator and improvement on mast-cell-directed treatment. The mean was 1.4
abnormal mediators per patient. The most common was prostaglandin D2 (37.5%); raised tryptase
is not among those listed. These criteria are not the only ones in use. Valent and 23 co-authors,
writing in 2019 and proposing a diagnostic algorithm, described overt MCAS as "an unusual condition" and warned that
"in several instances, symptoms are misinterpreted as MCA/MCAS" .
The present paper, by contrast, cites an estimated MCAS prevalence of 17%. Which criteria are
right is beyond this review. But the comparison group is defined by one clinic's reading of
contested criteria. And it was selected partly for having symptoms in many systems, the very
thing then counted in the comparison.

In Table 1, 68 women and 11 men add up to 79 MCAS patients, not 80.

## Outcome measures

All outcomes are self-reported symptoms. Under the guide, these carry the highest risk of
bias. Here, nothing protects against that: there is no blinding, no objective measure, and the
"before" data are recalled.

The central problem is what the MCMRS items measure. Reading the form (Appendix 4 and 1A), most
items are symptoms that any severe multisystem illness produces. They include: "significant
physical weakness or fatigue doing everyday activities", "extreme fatigue attacks", "brain fog",
"rapid heart rate", "sudden dizziness/lightheadedness", "shortness of breath", "cough",
migraine-like headaches, nerve pain or tingling, and worsening with sleep deprivation or
fasting. A smaller set points more specifically at mast cells: hives, flushing, swelling during
attacks, worsening with high-histamine foods, and itching around the anus during attacks.

Calling the total a measure of "mast cell activation symptoms" assumes the conclusion. A person
with ME/CFS, with heart failure, or recovering from any severe infection would score high on
most of these items. The study cannot tell "symptoms caused by mast cells" apart from "many
symptoms". It included no group with another chronic illness to test this.

The form itself shows how unspecific the score is. It says a sum of 14 or more means "diagnosis
of mast cell mediator release syndrome is clinically confirmed". The control group's mean was
9.7, with a standard deviation of 8.3. The paper does not report how many controls reached 14,
but with that mean and spread it is likely that a sizeable minority of hospital employees and
their spouses did.

The form's scoring also flattens several systems. For "eyes, ears, nose, mouth", "lungs" and
"hematologic", it gives one point if one or more items are present. The methods describe the
first score as the "total number of MCMRS symptoms", while Appendix 1A says "The scoring system
is shown below". It is not clear which of the two was used. The
laboratory, imaging and biopsy points that are part of the full MCMRS were not collected for
the Long Covid group or the controls. So the scores are not comparable to those the form was
designed around.

The Fatigue Assessment Scale is a validated 10-item fatigue measure. The self-rated health
score is a 0–100 line, like the visual scale in the EuroQol-5D (EQ-5D). Neither has a known
link to mast cells.

## Bias

**Recall bias.** The Long Covid group rated their pre-COVID-19 symptoms from memory, in the same
sitting as their current ones, after joining a Long Covid support group. People who are ill now
tend to remember their past health as better than it was, or to sharpen the contrast. The
MCAS patients recalled symptoms from up to two years earlier, before treatment. Their recruitment letter
said they would act "as a comparison to patients who have gotten very sick".

The authors say the "remarkably similar" pre-COVID-19 and control spider plots are "likely
negating this issue". That does not follow. A baseline that matches the controls is what you
would see if recall were accurate. It is also what you would see if people remembered
themselves as typical. The similarity cannot tell the two apart. And the baseline is not quite
the same as the controls'. The pre-COVID-19 system count was significantly higher than in
controls (6.1 against 5.3, p=0.01), and controls reported more brain fog (1.9 against 1.2).

**Recruitment framing.** The MCAS recruitment letter (Appendix 3) told patients: "we feel that
if your disease is well controlled, the risk of getting very sick from COVID-19 is lessened".
The control recruitment letter (Appendix 2) was framed around COVID-19 risk factors. The Long
Covid advert, the paper says, had "no other descriptors... that might bias recruitment". The
reader cannot check this, because the advert text is not in the appendices.

**Author preconceptions.** The authors set out to test their own hypothesis. In 2020, three of
them (Afrin, Weinstock and Molderings) published "Covid-19 hyperinflammation and post-Covid-19
illness may be rooted in mast cell activation syndrome" in the same journal. The discussion
reads this paper as support for a specific mechanism: new somatic mutations in mast cell stem
cells, caused by cytokine storms. Nothing in these data bears on that. The lead author
diagnosed all the MCAS patients, and the study was run from his practice. Weinstock and Afrin
are unpaid advisers to the start-up MC Sciences, Ltd., and Molderings is its chief medical
officer. The paper discloses these roles.

## Statistical and methodological issues

**Absence of a difference is treated as sameness.** The claim that Long Covid and MCAS
symptoms are "virtually identical" rests on non-significant t-tests between the two groups. A
non-significant difference is not evidence of equivalence. That would need an equivalence test
with a margin set in advance, and there is none. With 80 MCAS patients and wide standard
deviations, sizeable differences could go undetected.

**The item-level results contradict the "virtually identical" claim.** For 21 of 51 symptoms,
post-COVID-19 Long Covid and MCAS patients differed significantly. Look at which symptoms
differ. MCAS patients scored higher on most of the items that point most specifically at mast
cells: hives (2.9 against 1.3), flushing (3.8 against 2.2), worsening with high-histamine foods
(5.4 against 3.2), itching around the anus during attacks (1.9 against 0.6) and skin nodules.
They also scored higher on abdominal pain, cramping, diarrhoea and nausea. The Long Covid group
scored higher on physical weakness, fatigue attacks, muscle pain, chest pain, cough, sweats,
and loss of smell and taste. The groups differ on most of the specific items, while many of
the unspecific ones are similar. That pattern is what you would expect if Long Covid raises general symptom
burden rather than producing an MCAS-like picture. The authors attribute the higher abdominal
scores in MCAS patients to the clinic being a gastroenterology one. That may be part of it,
but it does not explain hives, flushing or histamine-food sensitivity.

The quality of life comparison also goes against the claim. Long Covid participants rated their
health much lower than MCAS patients did before treatment (40.5 against 56.6, p<0.0001).

**No correction for multiple comparisons.** Each of 51 symptoms was tested three times, plus
the summary scores, at p ≤ 0.05. About 8 of the roughly 150 symptom tests would come out
"significant" by chance alone. For the large before-and-after changes this does not matter;
they are very large. It matters more for the handful of baseline differences and the
borderline between-group results.

**An impossible test statistic.** The paper reports the before-and-after change in MCMRS score
(11.4, standard deviation 7.5, to 21.1, standard deviation 6.3) as t(135) = −6.1, p = 0.0001.
With these means and standard deviations, the standard deviation of the paired differences
cannot exceed 7.5 + 6.3 = 13.8. So t must be at least about 8.2. Even at t = 6.1, p would be far
below 0.0001. The comparable symptom count change, with almost the same means and spreads, is
reported as t = −16.5. The direction of the finding is not in doubt, but this is a reporting
error.

**Analyses described but not reported.** The methods say "Multivariant analysis was performed
to determine if any clinical characteristic, medicine, or supplement altered the prevalence or
severity of MCA symptoms in LC." No results of it appear anywhere in the paper. Table 2 shows
large rises in antihistamine (36.0% to 56.6%), immunosuppressant (7.4% to 29.4%) and vitamin
C and D use after COVID-19. The paper does not say whether these were linked to symptom scores.

**No adjustment for sex or body mass index**, although both differed between groups.

**The spider plots** (Figure 3) normalise each system to 0–100%. This makes the shapes look
alike, but they are visual summaries with no test attached. Viewed as images, the post-COVID-19
and MCAS plots differ in places: the Long Covid plot is larger on lungs, neurologic and
constitutional, the MCAS plot on skin and urine/pelvis.

## Biological plausibility

Mast cells may well play some part in Long Covid. But this study cannot test it. The questions
it asks would be answered "yes" by most people with a disabling post-infectious illness,
whatever the mechanism. A useful test would ask whether something specific to mast cells is
present: raised mediators measured properly, or a symptom pattern that sets Long Covid apart
from other post-viral illness on the items specific to mast cells. On the specific items, this
paper's own data point the other way.

The guide's "natural experiment" test applies. Conditions in which no one suspects mast cells
would very probably score high on this questionnaire too. Severe depression, ME/CFS of
non-COVID origin, and recovery from a severe illness of any kind are examples. No such
comparison group was included.

The proposed mechanisms in the discussion are the authors' prior hypotheses. These include
stressor-induced cytokine storms causing new somatic mutations in mast cell progenitors. None
is tested here. The paper admits that "detection of somatic mutations in mast cells is not
available in any clinical laboratories".

## Interpretation and spin

The abstract says: "Post-COVID-19 LC subjects and MCAS patients prior to treatment had
virtually identical MCA symptom and severity analysis." The discussion says: "The symptom data
and spider web plots illustrated that LC patients' symptoms are virtually identical to those
experienced by MCAS patients." Neither statement fits the paper's own result that 21 of 51
symptoms differed significantly, or the large difference in quality of life.

The title says "Mast cell activation symptoms are prevalent in Long-COVID". This takes the
questionnaire's label as a description of cause. What was shown is that people in Long Covid
support groups report many symptoms. Most of them are not specific to mast cells.

The abstract's conclusion goes further: "Increased activation of aberrant mast cells induced by
SARS-CoV-2 infection by various mechanisms may underlie part of the pathophysiology of LC". No
mast cell activity was measured in anyone with Long Covid.

To the authors' credit, the discussion contains real hedges. "These results support, but do
not provide definitive proof of, our earlier hypothesis". And: "we are not equating LC with a
mutual diagnosis of MCAS since diagnosis of MCAS compliant with published criteria requires
testing not pursued in the LC participants". It also notes that mast cell symptoms "can be
present purely due to normal reactivity". These are fair statements. The abstract and title do
not carry them.

The discussion then reports that "We have seen improvement in our LC patients in our clinics"
with a long list of mast-cell-directed treatments, citing mostly acute-COVID-19 studies and
hypothesis papers. This is anecdote and has no place as support for the findings.

## What this demonstrates

It shows that people recruited from Long Covid support groups in 2020–2021 reported, at one
sitting, far more symptoms now than they recalled having before infection. They also reported
much worse fatigue and self-rated health. The size of that change is large and is unlikely to
be wholly an artefact, though recall bias inflates it by an unknown amount. It also shows that
the overall burden of these particular symptoms was of the same order as that recalled by one
clinic's MCAS patients.

It does not show that mast cells are activated in Long Covid. It does not show that the
symptom pattern is MCAS-like rather than generally multisystemic: on the items specific to
mast cells, the groups differed. It does not show that mast cell treatments would help.

## Critical missing elements

- Any measurement of mast cell mediators, tryptase or tissue mast cells in the Long Covid group.
- A disease control group with a non-mast-cell chronic illness, ideally post-viral illness after
  another infection, or ME/CFS of non-COVID origin.
- A definition of Long Covid, illness duration and time since infection.
- ME/CFS, POTS and post-exertional malaise status in the Long Covid group, and POTS status in
  the MCAS group.
- An equivalence analysis for the claim of similarity.
- The results of the multivariable analysis the methods describe.
- Adjustment for sex.
- The text of the Long Covid advert.

## Recommendations

**For readers.** This paper is often cited as evidence that mast cell activation underlies
Long Covid. It is evidence that people with Long Covid report many symptoms that also appear
on an MCAS checklist. That is a much weaker claim. It does not justify an MCAS diagnosis or
treatment for Long Covid on its own.

**For researchers.** Measure mediators properly in Long Covid and matched controls, with
attention to sample handling. Compare the symptom checklist against other post-viral and
chronic illness groups, to see whether it picks out anything specific. Test mast-cell-directed
treatments in placebo-controlled trials rather than inferring benefit from clinic experience.

## Final assessment

The study answers a simple descriptive question with a questionnaire, and the descriptive
answer is believable in direction: Long Covid support group members report many more symptoms
than before infection. The problem is the step from there to mast cells. The questionnaire is
mostly made of symptoms common to any disabling multisystem illness. There is no
non-mast-cell disease group to show otherwise. The baseline is recalled. The comparison group
was selected partly for having many affected systems. And the claim of being "virtually
identical" to MCAS rests on non-significant tests. That claim is contradicted by the paper's
own item-level data, which split on most of the symptoms specific to mast cells.

What prevents a higher score: the design cannot separate "mast cell activation" from "being
ill with many symptoms", which is the paper's central inference. The title and abstract
overstate even the descriptive result. There are also smaller reporting problems: an
impossible t-value, an unreported multivariable analysis, and counts that do not add up.

What prevents a lower score: the data do not contradict the narrow claim that symptom burden
rose and reached MCAS-clinic levels. Methods and questionnaires are reported openly enough to
check, which is how these problems can be seen. The discussion contains real caveats, even if
the abstract drops them.

## Executive summary

People with Long Covid were recruited through Facebook support groups and answered a
questionnaire about their symptoms. They answered for now, and from memory for before their
COVID-19 infection, in one sitting. Their answers were compared with hospital staff and their
spouses, and with patients one doctor had diagnosed with mast cell activation syndrome (MCAS),
who recalled their symptoms from before treatment. The questionnaire is a checklist used to
screen for MCAS.

The Long Covid group recalled having about as many symptoms as the staff before infection, and
reported about twice as many now. That was similar to the MCAS patients' total. The authors
conclude that mast cell activation symptoms are common in Long Covid, and that Long Covid
symptoms are "virtually identical" to MCAS.

Most items on the checklist are not specific to mast cells: tiredness, weakness, brain fog,
fast heart rate, dizziness, shortness of breath, headaches. Almost any disabling illness would
score high on them. The study did not include people with another illness to check this. On
most of the few items that do point at mast cells, the groups differed: MCAS patients reported
more hives, flushing and reactions to high-histamine foods. Long Covid patients reported more
fatigue attacks, weakness, cough and chest pain. Overall, 21 of 51 symptoms differed, and Long Covid
patients rated their health much worse. No one in the Long Covid group had any test of mast
cell activity. The "before" answers depend on memory. There are also reporting errors. The
three authors advise or work for a start-up company, MC Sciences, and disclose it.

The study shows that people with Long Covid report many symptoms. It does not show that mast
cells cause them.

Grade: 3.5 / 10.
